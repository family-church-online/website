# Beloved of Jesus

So the crucifixion of Jesus through the eyes of John the Apostle. One John, chapter one, verses one to four. That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we looked upon and have touched with our hands, concerning the life. The life was made manifest, and we've seen it, and testify to it and proclaim to you the eternal life which was with the Father and was made manifest to us. That which we have seen and heard, we proclaim also to you so that you too may have fellowship with us. And indeed, our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son, Jesus Christ. And we are writing these things so that our joy may be complete. John's mind, swirling in exhausted shock, drifted. It was as if he was standing at Lazarus' tomb again. Jesus said that Lazarus was sleeping and that he would have to go and wake him up. And he said something about twelve hours of daylight and how those walking in it wouldn't stumble, but nobody understood. And Lazarus was dead. And he had been for four days. John could see it like it was in front of him, Jesus weeping, the wailing of mourners pushing back all the sound before silence descended. As Jesus told them, Take away the stone. His words seemed to resonate back and forth among the graves as the confusion that they'd caused settled in. Nothing seemed right in that graveyard. Everything felt contaminated, even the scent of the wildflowers and the damp evening air. And the thought of releasing the stench of death was too much. But the look on Jesus' face quickly hushed any objections. The silence slowly crumbled under the grinding echo of the massive stone. Everyone held their breath as it rolled to its rest. The same sound shook John into the present as a huge stone rolled into place over the entrance to Jesus' tomb. How could this be happening? When he finally managed to tear his eyes away from the tomb, his gaze dropped to his feet. He stared at them and his thoughts drifted again to the Passover just hours before. Jesus had washed these feet and lovingly dried them with a towel wrapped around his waist, but they were dirty now. Everything felt dirty now. He looked up again expecting the stone to roll back and thinking about Jesus' voice that thundered, Lazarus, come out. Lazarus had walked out of the grave. Why couldn't Jesus? All he saw was the devastation in the eyes of Joseph and Nicodemus as they walked away, having hastily embalmed the body in a race against the falling darkness. And then the sadistic taunt of the priest rang in John's ears. He saved others. Why doesn't he save himself? John's thoughts returned to the week before. They'd eaten with Lazarus and Mary and Martha. Mary had anointed Jesus with an expensive perfume, and its smell seemed to return with the memory of his words. Jesus said, Leave her alone, that she may keep it for the day of my burial, for the poor you always have with you, but you do not always have me. How could this be his burial? He's the resurrection and the life. But John had seen him die through the blur of tears, from the sound of the hammer's shill, metallic assault, to the awful sight of the blood and water gushing from the stab wound in Jesus' side. This is my body for you. This is my blood of the covenant. Blood had been everywhere, all day. John felt as if he was covered in it. He turned to leave. Oh, how he needed someone to fall on, to hold him up. Every step felt closer to collapse. He could still feel the warmth of Jesus' chest against his cheek when he'd reclined against him at the table to ask who would betray him. The swirling shadows of the evening brought back the memory of the soldiers and the garden and Judas, the son of destruction. John remembered Jesus' hands breaking the bread, dipping it, and handing it to Judas. No one dreamed it would end like this. Violent anger burned in John, the son of thunder, as Jesus had called him. Those same hands that had lifted the lame to their feet and opened the eyes of the blind now lay torn and disfigured behind the cold stone wall of Joseph's tomb. This isn't right. It's not fair. Someone has to pay. Who will pay the price for this brutal injustice? Anger gave way to concern. John needed to get Mary home. Jesus had given him his mother and the responsibility of caring for her as his own. Mary and a number of the women were standing away off, watching, weeping, wailing and then completely falling apart. Their despair took John's mind back to Jairus' wife, the mother of that little twelve year old girl, who had died while they waited for Jesus. She'd also been inconsolable and broken in disbelief. Before, in the quiet of her home, Jesus had calmly taken the child's hand and raised her from the dead by his words. He saved others. He was always saving others. How could they do this to him? How he wished he knew how to console Mary. How John wished someone would bring back her son. Everywhere he looked, he saw something that reminded him of Jesus. He remembered his voice calling him from his father's fishing boats, the abandoned joy of picking up and leaving, leaving everything to be with Jesus and to travel the length and breadth of Israel. He also remembered the dazzling white garments that enthroned Jesus on the mountain as they heard the voice of God and saw Moses and Elijah talking to Jesus about his departure. I couldn't understand that at the time, but now it made sense. Moses and Elijah knew what it was like to walk to their own death. Jesus knew. Of course, he knew. He did try he did try to tell John and the others over and over again how John wished he'd listened closer, paid more attention, appreciated Jesus more while he could. The love of Jesus had made John make sense of life. It made more sense to him than anything else. It was the love of Jesus that defined John, gave him his identity, gave his life meaning. But how could life have meaning now? He was afraid to close his eyes. The horror of the crucifixion, the cruelty of the crown of thorns, and the torn flesh on Jesus' back thrust against the splintered wooden cross shocked him back to reality, to the present. He'd never wept like this, and it felt like this crushing sadness would consume him. If it was hard to keep his eyes open, the exhaustion that weighed so heavily on him tempted them closed, as did everything he looked at. The sights of Jerusalem brought painful waves of memories to the mind of the disciple that Jesus loved. Those memories brought more questions. What did all of this mean? What did Jesus mean when he said, truly, truly I say to you, you will weep and lament, but the world will rejoice. You'll be sorrowful, but the sorrow you feel will turn to joy. When a woman is giving birth, she has sorrow because her hour has come. But when she's delivered the baby, she no longer remembers the anguish for the joy that a human being has been born into the world. So also, you will have sorrow now, but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you. That didn't seem possible right now, but then Jesus loves the impossible. John's questions burned and writhed in his heart as he held Mary and they walked off into the night. John's questions should be our questions today. His first question, he saved others. Why didn't he save himself? Peter later wrote, For Christ also suffered once for sin, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the Spirit. Paul said that being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on the cross. Therefore, God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father. Who's to blame? Who should pay the price for this brutal injustice? John himself wrote, This is love. Not that we have loved God, but that he has loved us and sent his Son as an atoning sacrifice for our sin. We put him there. It's our fault just as much as it's Judas and the Pharisees. As we read when we started the service today, Isaiah, many centuries before this awful day, this wonderful day, wrote, Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows, yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted. He was pierced for our transgressions. He was crushed for our iniquities. Upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace. And with his wounds, we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray. We've turned, every one of us, to his own way, and the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all. The third question, how can life have meaning now? John couldn't see it that day, but he came to see it. And he wrote for us, Dear friends, since God so loved us, we ought also to love one another. No one's ever seen God, but if we love one another, God lives in us and his love is made complete in us. We know that we live in him and he in us because he has given us his Spirit. And we have seen and testify that the Father has sent his Son to be the savior of the world. My dear children, I write this to you so that you will not sin. But if anybody does sin, we have one who speaks to the Father in our defense: Jesus Christ, the Righteous One. He's the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not only for our sins, but also for the sins of the world.
