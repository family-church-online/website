# Lukewarm

### The Church Age and Its Purpose

We continue with the book of Revelation, Revelation chapter 3, verses 14 to 22.

And to the angel of the church in Laodicea write, The words of the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's creation: I know your works. You are neither cold nor hot. Would you were either cold or hot. So because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I will spit you out of my mouth. For you say, 'I am rich, I have prospered,' not realizing that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind and naked. So I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, that you may be rich in white garments, so that you may clothe yourselves, and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen. And serve to anoint your eyes so that you may see. Those whom I love I reprove and discipline. So be zealous and repent. Behold, I stand at the door and knock. And if anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to be with him, and eat with him, and he with me. The one who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my throne, as I also conquered, and sat down with my Father on his throne. He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

This is the last time you'll hear this little introduction. The reason it's the last time is we're now talking about the last of the seven churches. These letters were dictated to John while he was on the isle of Patmos by Jesus Christ directly, and were given to messengers who took them to the seven churches of Asia Minor.

Revelation is about the things as they are now and the things to come. This period is referred to as the Church Age. This Church Age started in Acts 2 when Jesus poured out his Holy Spirit on the believers. The Church is composed of those who, by faith alone, through God's grace alone, accept Jesus Christ as our Lord and Savior. Christ is the head of this church, as he is of every single church—the church universal.

This local church, Family Church, where we as believers get together, despite the cold, to worship you, God—to teach one another, to encourage one another, to support one another, to build one another up in the knowledge and grace of Jesus Christ. That's what we do. Jesus wants us to do that, and he's the head of that. You can't really be a Christian on your own. You have to be a Christian in Christian community so that we're knit together with all the gifts God's given us and create a much bigger whole than what we would possibly be on our own.

All the churches—hundreds of thousands, if not millions today—that are meeting and giving praise to you, God. That's known as the universal church. As we see in Revelation, foretold in the New Testament in many places like 1 Thessalonians 4 and 1 Corinthians 15, this church age will end. When it ends, all God's people, including those that came before us, are raptured out of this world and taken to be with our Lord.

### Laodicea's Wealth and Water Supply

Let's get into this last letter to the church of Laodicea. There are a couple of things about Laodicea that Jesus will use in a play on words in his message to the Laodiceans.

It was a wealthy city on the main trade route from the west coast of Asia Minor to the road leading inland. They were well known for breeding sheep that produced a glossy black wool. This glossy black wool was sought after throughout Asia Minor because you could make very fine, expensive clothing out of it, particularly coats.

There was also a medical school in Laodicea where physicians developed a very effective eye salve. The key ingredient is boric acid, which is still used in eye wash today.

The third thing we need to know about the Laodiceans is that despite the size of that city, they lacked fresh drinking water. The water was piped in from around ten kilometers away via a sophisticated aqueduct system. It came from a hot spring ten kilometers away, and by the time it reached Laodicea, it was lukewarm. Lukewarm becomes the essence of this whole message. There's a practical thing that Jesus uses as a metaphor for his message.

### Who Jesus Is

Jesus announces himself to this church in Laodicea. He draws from Revelation 1:12-18. Every time he speaks to the churches, he tells them who he is. That's why we have songs of worship and praise, why we seek to understand who it is we worship here today.

The words he uses to the Laodiceans are "the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's creation." First, he announces himself as "the Amen." We know what "Amen" means—we use it at the end of our prayers. What does it mean? Amen means "so be it." The word also means "truly" and "verily." We often read in the Bible that Jesus, when he speaks to his disciples, says, "Verily, verily, I say unto you." Verily is an old-fashioned adverb meaning "in truth." When Jesus says "the words of the Amen," he means "I am the truth." I'm unchangeable. I'm true all the time and all the promises that I make are true. I myself am the affirmation of this truth. Paul says in 2 Corinthians 1, "For all the promises of God in him are yes, and in him Amen, to the glory of God."

The second part of his announcement is "I'm the faithful and true witness," reinforcing this concept of truth. Jesus says, "My words are reliable and true. My testimony is accurate." This is summed up in John 14:6: "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through him." This is the message given to us by God.

The last of the announcements is "the beginning of God's creation." I can't say it any better than Paul did in Colossians 1:16: "For by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities. All things were created through him and for him."

### The Majesty of Jesus

These are massively profound statements. When I read this, I tend to take these statements for granted because I become accustomed to them. I get desensitized to the praise and glory the Psalmists give to God. Even when we sing songs, we go through the motions. We don't truly understand who we're worshipping and what it means.

I'm reminded of John, the person to whom these letters were first given. He walked as a disciple with Jesus. He walked along the dusty roads of Galilee and beyond. Jesus was his teacher and also his friend. He saw Jesus on the Mount of Transfiguration, as we read in the gospel. He even leaned on Jesus' head at the Last Supper. And you remember, at the Last Supper he prevailed with his mother to come to Jesus and ask that he and his brother sit on either side of Jesus. Jesus looked at them and said, "You don't know what you're asking. You just don't know what you're asking." Just continue to serve. And he didn't know. He didn't really appreciate what Jesus was all about. I suppose because mostly Jesus' real divine power was hidden behind his human form. John certainly would have learned much more about Jesus's majesty at the resurrection and after.

He lived the longest, nearly to a hundred years, and spent all that time after Jesus' Ascension building the early church. He must have gained some clear idea as to who Jesus was. But in Revelation 1, when John encountered Jesus, he heard Jesus speaking and turned toward him. What he saw overwhelmed him to the extent that he fell onto his face as though he were dead.

Here was a person who probably had the clearest understanding of who Jesus was, yet he was absolutely overwhelmed when he saw Jesus in his glorified state. When Jesus speaks to us, as he's speaking to the Laodiceans, we must listen because he is the power beyond all powers.

This is illustrated in Philippians 2:10-11: "At the name of Jesus every knee will bow in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue will acknowledge that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." This indicates what will happen at Jesus's second coming. Those of us who have responded to the gospel with faith and repentance will happily acknowledge that every knee will bow and every tongue will proclaim. But those who've hardened their hearts to the call of the gospel will do so in fear and trembling. Why? Because it's too late. When Jesus speaks, we better listen.

### Lukewarmness and Spiritual Indifference

When we listen to what he said to the Laodiceans, we see that there was no commendation. This is the only church of the seven that received no commendation. Clearly what they are doing is not acceptable. He starts straight away with the chastisement in verses 15 and 16.

He says, "I know your works. You are neither cold nor hot. Would you were either cold or hot. So because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I will spit you out of my mouth." Some translations say "vomit."

Here Jesus speaks metaphorically, using the analogy of the water that comes in, which when lukewarm is not acceptable. Why is lukewarm not acceptable? If we look at it in its literal sense, water that is lukewarm is not really that useful. I mean, who wants to climb into a lukewarm bath? And those of us that are into the coffee culture, who wants to drink a lukewarm cup of coffee?

But in a much broader sense, when you are lukewarm, it means you lack enthusiasm, passion, and commitment. You're half-hearted in your response to an idea. In this sense, you're spiritually indifferent. Doesn't matter if I come to church. Doesn't matter if I don't speak to anybody. Doesn't matter if I don't console somebody. Doesn't matter if I don't agree with what somebody said. Doesn't matter. That's what lukewarm means. You're simply going through the motions. You come to church out of habit. I hope there's nobody here that's come today simply because that's what you do on a Sunday morning. Jesus says that if you're lukewarm, you're not acceptable in the church.

### Wealth and Spiritual Blindness

And he says to the Laodiceans, "You say, 'I am rich and I have prospered,' not realizing that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind and naked."

There's nothing wrong—and we hear this all the time—with being wealthy and successful. The problem is that you find that state more attractive than God, so that your material wealth and comfort become your god. And that happens so easily and so quickly. And that's what happened in Laodicea. And we can see that today.

Those who are not wealthy are chasing wealth, and that has become their god. They work eighteen hours a day, forego their family responsibilities, and don't come to church because Sunday is the day they can sleep at least. They have made that their god. They are probably worse than lukewarm, but they certainly at best are lukewarm.

In 60 AD, just before this letter was written, an earthquake destroyed Laodicea. Most of those affected would still have been alive when this letter was read to the church. Rome stepped in with aid money to help restore their economy for rebuilding. The Laodiceans said, "No thanks. We have sufficient resources of our own to fund the reconstruction of our town."

You can imagine their attitude: they were self-important, they were counting on their own resources. If you're in this state, it's fertile ground for self-satisfaction, self-fulfillment, contentment with your own achievements—pride. And we know pride is one of the cardinal sins. You get smug and lazy. You get blinded to your own faults, the inevitable consequence of being captain of your own ship.

I've worked hard to understand the consequences of this state of mind. You have absolutely no proper understanding of your own fallen and sinful nature, because pride is standing in the way. You don't understand the consequences of that. You're no longer touched by the word of God. There are many in this church who, when we sing and verses from the Bible are read, feel something stir inside. The Spirit inside you is touched by God's Holy Spirit, and that word means something. If you're in the state that the Laodiceans find themselves, you're no longer touched by the Word of God. The Bible is not a guiding light in your life.

Christian fellowship, for example, is no longer a priority. Perhaps you come to church and when it's finished you're gone, not to be seen. Christian fellowship is, as we said right at the outset, absolutely cardinal for us to live out our faith. You never think to share and witness to others about your faith. Why? Because you've got nothing to share, and it's not even on your priority list. You'd rather talk about the latest deal you're working on.

You cannot see that your material wealth is transient. You can't get it into perspective. It's foolish to put your trust in that. Matthew 6 and James 5 say, "Rather lay up treasures in heaven, for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Where your treasure is, inevitably, that's where your heart will be.

### The Gold and Garments

Jesus continues in verse 18: "I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, that you may be rich. White garments, so that you may clothe yourselves, and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen. Salve to anoint your eyes, so that you may see."

He uses the metaphor of clothing that they would be familiar with. You're well clothed with this wool that you produce and amongst many other things, but you're naked. The emperor with no clothes, taking yourself so seriously. You clean your eyes and think you're fine, but in fact you're blind.

When I was thinking about this and read that Jesus said "I counsel you to buy from me," I thought, why, when speaking about wealth and buying, would Jesus say "buy from me"? After praying on this, it became clear that Jesus is using that word ironically. When you speak ironically, you use a word to mean the opposite of what you're saying. What you'll buy from God will cost you—specifically, your pride and your self-reliance. So in a sense, you are buying it because it's costing you. And that's exactly what he's saying.

By the way, why does he talk about gold? It's a common biblical metaphor for godly character. So Jesus says, "Buy from me a godly character."

### Called to Zealous Repentance

He continues: "Those whom I love I reprove and discipline. So be zealous and repent."

Now isn't this just absolutely magnificent? Here God pours out his disdain to those in Laodicea, yet he still says, "I love you. I want the best for you." He calls on them to repent because he's got such great love for them. The verse John 3:16 says, "God so loved the world that he gave his only begotten Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." That's how much he loves us.

He says, "Be zealous." Zealous simply means that you've got to act immediately. Don't wait. And what should you be zealous about? You should be zealous about repenting.

This is another word we often forget the meaning of. Let's put ourselves in the minds of the Laodiceans. If they were to repent, they would have to come to the recognition that this thing called sin is actually in them. God's Holy Spirit moves in our spirit to convict us of our sin. God actually initiates that. If you harden your heart, God won't speak to you. Along the way, he will turn away. The Laodiceans won't be able to understand this: compared to my peers, perhaps I'm fine, but in relation to God, I'm a sinner. I might be better than my peers. In fact, I might even pride myself that I'm better than my peers. But when it comes to godliness, I'm a sinner. Inherently I have sinned. And the more that I actually think about it, the more I'm aware that I am actually a sinner. And the little things I take for granted are sins.

You realize that if you continue in this course, you're bound for eternal punishment—separation from God. You enter a state of zealousness—exactly what Jesus called this church to—where you want to escape that present state. You want to resolve to turn back on your sin, to deny yourself, to take up the cross, and to commit yourself to Jesus, despite the fact that many people might scorn you. It doesn't matter. The words keep ringing in my ears: they don't know what we know. Non-Christians don't know what we know. And we know that Jesus welcomes you into his arms as a result of that.

So Jesus is saying, "Repent. Do what we've discussed, and do it quickly." That's his message to the church: Repent and do it fast.

### Jesus Knocking at the Door

And then he carries on to say, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. And if anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me."

This is a very well-known picture. Many of you know that Christians often speak of "Jesus knocking on the door of your heart," which is typically about an unbeliever's heart. And I think that's taken from here. And while that might be quite a good picture, it's not what's represented in Revelation. If someone claims this message appears in Revelation, that's incorrect. God's talking to the unsaved church, and he's saying, "I'm knocking on the door and I'm waiting for you to answer."

I remember as a little boy in Sunday school, we received picture cards with verses and had to memorize them. And next Sunday when we came back, we had to stand up and say it from memory. And there was a famous picture on this little thing that I had. I didn't realize how famous it was until I looked it up. In the 1850s, a painting called "The Light of the World" by William Hunt captured this verse. Some of you might be familiar with it.

Jesus stands with his flowing robes, holding a lantern, before a huge wooden door. He's waiting for someone to open it because, in the picture, there's no knob on the door. So Jesus couldn't open it. He was waiting for somebody to open it to let him in.

And of course, this touches on a very central debate that's taken place in the Christian community for two thousand years. I don't want to detail it now because it would take us down a rabbit hole that's not appropriate. But it's to do with predestination and free will, election and free will.

Ephesians 2 says: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not of your own doing. It is the gift of God, not as a result of works, so that none of you may boast."

So we are saved purely because God has chosen to elect us. Yet humans are still held accountable when we stand before God for whether we've accepted Jesus Christ. Here's a picture of Jesus knocking on the door, waiting for the door to be opened.

Jesus says, "He who has an ear, let him hear."

Most people, I think, believe this is a mystery we can't solve. There's a bit of both. And we accept that this mystery is one that we will have clarified one day when we stand face to face with Jesus. But for the moment, we understand that the primary mover in our salvation is God, but there's an element of choice we must make. Regardless, Jesus is saying, "Repent—quickly."

### The Promise to Overcomers

And the result of doing this is unbelievable.

Jesus says, "The one who overcomes—I will grant him to sit with me on my throne, as I also conquered and sat down at my Father's throne."

He finishes, "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches."

In conclusion, let us remember who we worship. There is nobody else, there's nothing else in this whole world that is worthy of this worship. It is the person who created the world, who created us, who will dissolve this world, who will still be there, and will care for us forever and a day. So let's listen to what God has said to us.

Those of us who haven't repented should do so quickly. Let us not fall into the terrible dilemma the Laodiceans faced—allowing pride to take central place. Pride doesn't necessarily come from wealth. There are many reasons we can be full of pride and unable to see who we're actually worshipping.

### Closing Prayer

As we close our eyes in prayer, let us come before God and ask that he would continually enlighten us, God, by your power, that we might understand the absolute magnificence of your redemption plan for us. It's a plan that no human mind could have worked out. It's a plan of absolute love and grace.

And we are so thankful, Lord, that we are the recipients of that grace. We're grateful that you hold us in your arms, that we've been adopted into your family, that one day we'll witness you in all your magnificence—the magnificence that made John fall on his face in awe. We will be there, and we will see that forever and ever. Amen.
