pointNumber: 2
statement: >-
  It's easy to treat "ask, seek, knock" as three interchangeable ways of
  saying "keep praying," or to stop at asking and never move to seeking and
  acting, or to ask once and give up the moment an answer is delayed. This
  lesson exists because Jesus actually taught a connected pattern — prayer
  first, then discernment, then courageous and persistent action — and each
  part depends on trusting a Father who delights to give good gifts and does
  not tire of a persistent knock. Scripture points believers toward this
  fuller rhythm rather than passivity on one end or self-reliant striving on
  the other.
point: >-
  Jesus teaches one threefold pattern for approaching the Father, not three
  separate techniques: ask in humble dependence before deciding anything
  yourself, seek by opening your eyes to evaluate what God has placed in
  front of you, and knock by acting in courageous, sustained faith. He is
  not a reluctant giver — if imperfect human fathers know how to give good
  gifts, how much more will our heavenly Father give good gifts, even His
  own Spirit, to those who ask Him. And when the door does not open right
  away, knocking means persevering, trusting that prayer is shaping us even
  while we wait for it to change our circumstances.

    heading: "Jesus gives one threefold pattern, not three separate ideas"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Matthew 7:7-8"
        text: >-
          Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock,
          and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and
          the one who seeks finds, and to the one who knocks it will be
          opened.
    commentary: >-
      Jesus lays out a single connected process, not three interchangeable
      options, for determining what to do next. Asking comes first — with
      your eyes closed, on your knees, before you've made up your mind about
      anything. It's how every decision, spiritual or practical, should
      begin. Seeking comes next, and it requires opening your eyes:
      evaluating the real options in front of you, using the wisdom, gifts,
      and people God has already provided, rather than waiting passively for
      a sign to appear on the wall. Only then comes knocking — actually
      getting up and trying the door, which takes courage, because it's
      exactly the point where procrastination likes to step in and freeze us
      in the planning stage. Left undone, a decision prayed over but never
      acted upon isn't obedience; it's stalling with spiritual language
      attached. Jesus's pattern moves in one direction: pray, evaluate, act.

  - id: B
    heading: "The Father delights to give good gifts to those who ask"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Luke 11:11-13"
        text: >-
          What father among you, if his son asks for a fish, will instead of
          a fish give him a serpent; or if he asks for an egg, will give him
          a scorpion? If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts
          to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the
          Holy Spirit to those who ask him!
    commentary: >-
      The comparison Jesus draws is deliberately uncomfortable: even a
      flawed human father — the text says "evil," meaning simply imperfect
      and sinful — knows better than to hand his hungry child a snake
      instead of a fish, or a scorpion instead of an egg. If that baseline
      decency is true of ordinary fathers, the argument runs from lesser to
      greater: how much more will a perfect heavenly Father give good gifts
      to His children who ask? The point isn't only that God answers
      requests; it's that He gives Himself. The greatest gift promised here
      isn't a specific outcome but the Holy Spirit — God's own presence and
      power given to those who ask. Asking, then, isn't a one-time
      formality to be ticked off; it's a standing invitation to keep coming
      back to a Father who delights to give, not a reluctant one who has to
      be worn down.

  - id: C
    heading: "Knocking means persevering until the door opens"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Luke 18:1"
        text: >-
          And he told them a parable to the effect that they ought always to
          pray and not lose heart.
    commentary: >-
      If seeking requires open eyes, knocking requires open hands — a
      willingness to keep trying the door even when it doesn't open right
      away. Jesus told the parable of the persistent widow specifically so
      His disciples would always pray and not lose heart, and Bartimaeus
      embodies exactly that kind of prayer: a blind beggar shouted for
      Jesus, was told to be quiet, and simply shouted louder until Jesus
      called him forward. Perseverance in prayer isn't only about
      repetition; it's about overcoming whatever tries to silence the cry,
      whether that's a crowd, a season of delay, or self-doubt. And
      perseverance does something prayer requests alone cannot: it changes
      the one who prays. A woman who prayed for her husband's salvation for
      fifty or sixty years wasn't just waiting for an answer across those
      decades — her own faith was being built and deepened the entire time.
      Knocking, then, is never wasted, even while the door stays shut.
