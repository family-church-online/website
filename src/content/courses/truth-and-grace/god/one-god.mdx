statement: >-
  We believe (1) in one God who eternally exists in three co-equal persons -
  the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; (2) that though each divine person
  is fully and equally God, They differ in relationship towards each other and
  Their work in the world; (3) that this triune God is perfect and infinite in
  His being and attributes - an all-holy, all-loving, and all-wise Sovereign;
  (4) that from before creation, God determined to save sinners through the
  sacrifice of His Son, to govern all history according to His sovereign plan
  and to finally bring all things under one head, even Christ; and (5) that
  all things exist from Him, through Him, and for Him, and are created to
  display the glory of His wonderful Name.

    heading: "The Lord alone is God: exclusive, incomparable, and one"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Deuteronomy 6:4"
        text: >-
          "Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one."
      - ref: "Isaiah 46:9"
        text: >-
          "Remember the former things of old; for I am God, and there is no
          other; I am God, and there is none like me."
      - ref: "1 Corinthians 8:6"
        text: >-
          "Yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things
          and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are
          all things and through whom we exist."
      - ref: "1 Timothy 2:5"
        text: >-
          "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and
          men, the man Christ Jesus."
    commentary: >-
      Together these scriptures establish the absolute monotheism that runs
      from Moses through the apostles. The Shema declares the Lord's singular,
      undivided being — not a monad standing alone, but a God of perfect
      eternal unity whose oneness is the bedrock of all worship. Isaiah
      reinforces this with sovereign clarity: God is not merely superior to
      all rivals, He is incomparable, self-existent, and unique in kind —
      every false god is an idol, but the living God simply is. Paul, writing
      into a culture saturated with competing deities, reaffirms this
      confession while remarkably weaving the Lord Jesus Christ into its very
      fabric: the Father is the source and goal of all existence, and the Son
      is the one through whom all things were made — not a contradiction of
      monotheism but a deepening of what divine oneness means. Paul then seals
      the argument in 1 Timothy: precisely because there is one God, there can
      be only one mediator. The unity of God and the uniqueness of Christ's
      mediation stand or fall together.

  - id: B
    heading: "Three persons distinctly present and named: the baptism and the Great Commission"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Matthew 3:16–17"
        text: >-
          "And when Jesus was baptized, immediately he went up from the water,
          and behold, the heavens were opened to him, and he saw the Spirit of
          God descending like a dove and coming to rest on him; and behold, a
          voice from heaven said, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well
          pleased.'"
      - ref: "Matthew 28:19"
        text: >-
          "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in
          the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."
    commentary: >-
      At Christ's baptism, all three persons of the Trinity are present and
      distinctly active in a single, unrepeatable moment: the Son is baptised,
      the Spirit descends visibly upon Him, and the Father speaks from heaven
      in loving affirmation. The three are not successive modes of God's
      existence, nor three separate gods — they are co-present, each acting in
      a manner appropriate to their distinct personhood, yet in perfect
      harmony as the one God. The Great Commission then crystallises this into
      the structure of the church's mission: Jesus issues the command to
      baptise in the singular name — not names — of the Father, Son, and Holy
      Spirit. The use of the singular name is at once monotheistic and
      Trinitarian. To be baptised into this one name is to be brought into
      covenant relationship with the triune God in the fullness of His being.
      That Jesus commands baptism into His own name alongside the Father and
      the Spirit places Him unambiguously on the divine side of reality and
      seals the Trinitarian structure of Christian faith and practice.

  - id: C
    heading: "Three persons co-operating in the one saving purpose: election, sanctification, and redemption"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "1 Peter 1:1–2"
        text: >-
          "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, to those who are elect exiles of
          the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia,
          according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in the
          sanctification of the Spirit, for obedience to Jesus Christ and for
          sprinkling with his blood: May grace and peace be multiplied to
          you."
      - ref: "Revelation 1:4–6"
        text: >-
          "John to the seven churches that are in Asia: Grace to you and peace
          from him who is and who was and who is to come, and from the seven
          spirits who are before his throne, and from Jesus Christ the
          faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings
          on earth. To him who loves us and has freed us from our sins by his
          blood and made us a kingdom, priests to his God and Father, to him
          be glory and dominion forever and ever. Amen."
    commentary: >-
      In a single salutation, Peter describes the salvation of God's people as
      a thoroughly Trinitarian work: the Father foreknows and elects, the
      Spirit sanctifies and sets apart, and the Son provides the atoning blood
      that seals the covenant. Each person of the Godhead plays a distinct and
      essential role in the one saving purpose. John's apostolic greeting
      draws grace and peace from all three persons — the eternal Father, the
      Spirit in His fullness, and Jesus Christ identified by His redemptive
      work and royal reign. The doxology that follows ascribes glory and
      dominion to Christ "forever and ever" — a form of praise reserved in
      Scripture for God alone — and the entire sweep of redemptive history is
      encompassed within this Trinitarian benediction. Together, these texts
      show that the doctrine of the Trinity is not an abstract philosophical
      puzzle imported into Scripture but a living reality experienced by every
      believer from the foundation of their salvation to its final
      consummation. The three persons never act in competition; they act in
      perfect unity toward the one purpose of redemption.

takeaways:
  - >-
    God is one. The Scripture's consistent witness, from Moses to the
    apostles, affirms absolute monotheism — there is no other God beside the
    Lord.
  - >-
    God is triune. The one God eternally exists as three distinct persons —
    Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each fully and equally God, yet
    differentiated in their relationships and roles.
  - >-
    The Trinity is revealed in redemption. The clearest windows into the
    Trinitarian nature of God are not found in philosophical argument but in
    the great events of salvation: Christ's baptism, His atoning death, the
    sending of the Spirit.
  - >-
    The three persons act together. From Peter's greeting to John's doxology,
    Scripture depicts the Father, Son, and Spirit as co-operating in perfect
    unity in election, sanctification, and redemption — never in competition,
    always in harmony.
  - >-
    Trinitarian faith shapes Trinitarian worship. The command to baptise in
    the singular name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit means that authentic
    Christian life is lived in relationship with God as He truly is — not a
    reduced or partial deity, but the fullness of the triune God.
