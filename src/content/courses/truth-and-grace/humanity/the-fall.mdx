statement: >-
  We believe (1) that in the beginning God sovereignly created all things
  visible and invisible and took delight in it all; (2) that He created
  humankind male and female as His image bearers, endowing each with a dignity
  of equal worth and calling to govern and care for His creation; (3) that
  human life is sacred to God and begins at conception; (4) that God created
  marriage to be an exclusive and lifelong union between a natural man and a
  natural woman, giving the man the responsibility of self-sacrificial and
  loving leadership and the woman the responsibility of compatible support;
  and (5) that the first man, Adam, sinned and thereby incurred the penalty of
  death, physical and spiritual, for all people, and as a consequence of his

    commentary: >-
      Together, these passages trace sin from its precise historical point of
      entry to its full expression in the human condition. Genesis 3 records
      not a myth but a real event — a deliberate act of distrust and
      disobedience in which Adam grasped at wisdom and autonomy apart from
      God. The immediate consequences — shame, hiding, and relational fracture
      — reveal that sin's first damage was to the relationship between
      humanity and God, before extending outward into pain, conflict, and the
      curse of physical death, affecting the whole created order. Genesis 6:5
      shows what this one act unleashed: by the time of Noah, God's assessment
      of the human heart is total and unsparing — not that some people do
      wicked things, but that every intention of the thoughts of every human
      heart is "only evil continually." The orientation of the will, the
      imagination, and the desires has been comprehensively bent away from
      God. Together these texts establish that sin's entry was a single
      historical act with catastrophic and universal consequences for human
      nature itself.

    heading: "Sin's reach is universal and total: every human being stands guilty and accountable before God"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Romans 3:10-19, 23"
        text: >-
          as it is written: "None is righteous, no, not one; no one
          understands; no one seeks for God. All have turned aside; together
          they have become worthless; no one does good, not even one." "Their
          throat is an open grave; they use their tongues to deceive." "The
          venom of asps is under their lips." "Their mouth is full of curses
          and bitterness." "Their feet are swift to shed blood; in their paths
          are ruin and misery, and the way of peace they have not known."
          "There is no fear of God before their eyes." Now we know that
          whatever the law says it speaks to those who are under the law, so
          that every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world may be held
          accountable to God… for all have sinned and fall short of the glory
          of God.
      - ref: "Romans 5:12, 17"
        text: >-
          Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and
          death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all
          sinned… For if, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through
          that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of
          grace and the free gift of righteousness reign in life through the
          one man Jesus Christ.
    commentary: >-
      Paul builds an airtight case for the universality and depth of sin, and
      then traces its origin to a single head. Romans 3 marshals a chain of
      Old Testament texts whose scope is deliberately total — "none," "no, not
      one," "all" — moving from the corruption of the inner person
      (understanding, will, desires) through to its outward expression in
      speech, violence, and the absence of the fear of God. Paul's purpose is
      not merely condemnation but to silence every claim to self-sufficiency
      before God, so that the whole world stands accountable and the need for
      the gospel becomes undeniable. Romans 5 then provides the causal
      account: death's universal dominion flows from the sin of one man, Adam,
      whose representative act made death the condition of all. Crucially,
      Paul introduces the great parallel — just as one man's trespass brought
      death to all, one Man's righteousness brings life to all who receive it.
      Original sin is therefore not pessimism for its own sake but the
      necessary premise of the gospel.

    commentary: >-
      These three texts illumine the interior reality of the inherited sinful
      nature and its consequences. Romans 7:9 captures the dynamic between the
      law and the sin that already dwells within: before the commandment
      comes, there is a naïve vitality, but the law reveals the sin already
      present and, in doing so, brings death. Sin is not merely an external
      force but an active power within human nature, seizing opportunity and
      producing the very transgression the law forbids. First Corinthians
      15:22 uses the crisp parallel of the two Adams to show that death's grip
      is not a consequence of individual errors alone but of solidarity with
      Adam as the representative head of the old humanity — "in Adam all die."
      Ephesians 2:3 makes the inherited nature explicit and personal: we were
      "by nature children of wrath" — not merely people who made bad choices,
      but persons whose whole orientation of body, mind, and will was bent
      away from God from the start, and who therefore stood under His
      righteous judgment along with the rest of mankind. Together these texts
      complete the doctrine: what Adam incurred comes to every person as an
      inherited nature, expressed in actual transgression, and resulting in
      real personal guilt before God.

takeaways:
  - >-
    Sin entered the world through a single historical act. Genesis 3 and
    Romans 5 together present Adam's transgression as a real event with
    universal consequences — not a myth explaining human weakness, but the
    historical turning point at which death entered God's good creation.
  - >-
    The corruption of human nature is total and comprehensive. Genesis 6:5 and
    Romans 3 leave no part of the human person unaffected by sin — not the
    will, the understanding, the desires, or the speech. This is what
    theologians call "total depravity": not that every person is as wicked as
    they could be, but that sin has corrupted every dimension of who we are.
  - >-
    Every person inherits both a sinful nature and incurs personal guilt.
    Ephesians 2:3 establishes that we are "by nature" children of wrath — the
    nature is inherited. Romans 3:23 and Romans 5:12 together show that this
    nature expresses itself in actual sins for which each person bears real
    personal responsibility.
  - >-
    Death — physical and spiritual — is sin's penalty. The judgments of
    Genesis 3, the spread of death in Romans 5, and Paul's "I died" in Romans
    7 all confirm that death in both its dimensions — separation from God and
    the dissolution of the body — is the direct consequence of Adam's sin and
    its inheritance in us.
  - >-
    The doctrine of original sin is the necessary foundation of the gospel.
    Romans 5:12 and 17 and 1 Corinthians 15:22 frame the fall not as the final
    word but as the dark backdrop against which Christ's redemption shines.
    Understanding what Adam's sin cost humanity is what makes the free gift of
    righteousness in Christ so magnificently good news.
