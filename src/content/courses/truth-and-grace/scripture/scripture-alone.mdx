point: >-
  We believe that Scripture alone is our sufficient and final authority in all
  matters of belief and practice.
statement: >-
  We believe (1) that the Holy Scriptures consist of the 66 books of the Old
  and New Testaments in their original writing; (2) that the Spirit of God so
  influenced the authors that these writings are the Word of God; (3) that
  because of the divine origin and inspiration of its words, the Scriptures
  are true in all that they affirm and are trustworthy and without error; (4)
  that these Scriptures are the complete and only written revelation of God to
  people; (5) that every translation that is faithful to the originals and
  responsibly interpreted clearly reveals those truths necessary for our
  salvation, godly living, and maturity in Christ; and (6) that Scripture
  alone is our sufficient and final authority in all matters of belief and
  practice.
sections:

    heading: "The faith once for all delivered: Scripture is the complete and sufficient norm"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Jude 1:3"
        text: >-
          Beloved, although I was very eager to write to you about our common
          salvation, I found it necessary to write appealing to you to contend
          for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints.
      - ref: "Luke 16:29–31"
        text: >-
          But Abraham said, 'They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear
          them.' And he said, 'No, father Abraham, but if someone goes to them
          from the dead, they will repent.' He said to him, 'If they do not
          hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they be convinced if
          someone should rise from the dead.'
    commentary: >-
      Together these two texts establish that the written Word of God is both
      complete and sufficient as the final authority for faith — surpassing
      even the most dramatic imaginable alternatives. In Jude 1:3, the phrase
      "once for all delivered" is theologically decisive: the faith entrusted
      to the saints has been given completely and finally, as a finished
      deposit to be contended for rather than supplemented. There is no room
      for an ongoing or developing authority that could stand alongside
      Scripture — the revelation has been made, and the task of every
      generation of believers is to guard and proclaim what has been received.
      In Luke 16:29–31, Christ constructs a parable in which Abraham declares
      the written Scriptures — Moses and the Prophets — to be sufficient for
      repentance and saving faith, and refuses the suggestion that even a
      resurrection appearance would be more persuasive or authoritative. The
      very thought that something beyond Scripture could serve as a higher or
      more compelling authority is explicitly dismissed. Taken together, these
      texts establish that Scripture is not one authority among several but
      the singular, complete, and sufficient written norm — final because God
      has spoken, sufficient because nothing that has been given falls short.

  - id: B
    heading: "Christ models and defends sola Scriptura: \"It is written\" against temptation and tradition"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Matthew 4:1–4"
        text: >-
          Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be
          tempted by the devil. And after fasting forty days and forty nights,
          he was hungry. And the tempter came and said to him, "If you are the
          Son of God, command these stones to become loaves of bread." But he
          answered, "It is written, 'Man shall not live by bread alone, but by
          every word that comes from the mouth of God.'"
      - ref: "Mark 7:9–13"
        text: >-
          And he said to them, "You have a fine way of rejecting the
          commandment of God in order to establish your tradition! For Moses
          said, 'Honor your father and your mother'; and, 'Whoever reviles
          father or mother must surely die.' But you say, 'If a man tells his
          father or his mother, "Whatever you would have gained from me is
          Corban"' (that is, given to God)— then you no longer permit him to
          do anything for his father or mother, thus making void the word of
          God by your tradition that you have handed down. And many such
          things you do."
    commentary: >-
      In Matthew 4:1–4 and Mark 7:9–13, Christ both models and defends the
      sole authority of Scripture — first in His own conduct, then in His
      rebuke of those who had departed from it. In the wilderness, facing the
      most intense personal temptation, the Son of God reaches for no other
      weapon than the written Word: It is written. He does not appeal to His
      own divine authority, to angelic support, or to personal spiritual
      insight. He submits to and cites Scripture as His final and decisive
      authority — establishing the pattern that every believer and every
      generation of the Church is to follow. In Mark 7:9–13, Christ exposes
      with severity the precise mechanism by which Scripture's authority is
      eroded: human tradition, accumulated over time and treated with growing
      reverence, is allowed to interpret and qualify the written Word until it
      is effectively nullified. The Pharisees had not abandoned Scripture
      openly — they had simply placed tradition alongside it until tradition
      won. Christ's rebuke is unambiguous: this is a rejection of the
      commandment of God. Together these two passages show that the sole
      authority of Scripture is not merely a Protestant slogan but the pattern
      of Christ Himself — and that the danger to that authority does not
      usually come from frontal attack but from the quiet encroachment of
      tradition, culture, and human opinion.

  - id: C
    heading: "Scripture governs the whole Christian life: written for our instruction in every age"
    scriptures:
      - ref: "Romans 15:4"
        text: >-
          For whatever was written in former days was written for our
          instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of
          the Scriptures we might have hope.
    commentary: >-
      Paul's statement is sweeping in its reach: whatever was written — the
      whole of the Old Testament — was written for the instruction of New
      Testament believers. This single verse reveals the intended scope of
      Scripture's authority: it is not limited to formal doctrinal questions
      but extends to the formation of endurance, the cultivation of hope, and
      the entire posture and disposition of the Christian life. If even the
      earlier portions of the canon, written centuries before, carry this
      weight for every subsequent generation, then the whole of God's word —
      Old and New Testaments together — stands as the sufficient and final
      authority over all that the people of God believe, practise, and hope
      for. Scripture's authority is not selective or partial; it is
      comprehensive, covering the full range of "all matters of belief and
      practice."

takeaways:
  - >-
    The faith has been "once for all delivered." Because the deposit of
    revealed truth is complete and final, Scripture — not ongoing tradition or
    new revelation — is the sufficient and settled authority for all that
    Christians are to believe and practise.
  - >-
    Christ Himself modelled submission to Scripture. When facing temptation
    and conflict, Jesus appealed to the written Word as His final authority —
    establishing the pattern for every believer and every generation of the
    Church.
  - >-
    Tradition must always be subject to Scripture, never its equal. Christ's
    rebuke of the Pharisees stands as a perpetual warning: human tradition,
    however ancient or respected, becomes destructive the moment it begins to
    displace or qualify the plain teaching of God's word.
  - >-
    No external authority exceeds the authority of Scripture. Even the
    testimony of one raised from the dead, in Christ's own parable, is placed
    beneath the authority of Moses and the Prophets — Scripture alone is God's
    sufficient word for repentance and faith.
  - >-
    Scripture governs the whole of the Christian life. Its authority extends
    beyond formal doctrine to encompass endurance, hope, moral practice, and
    the entire shape of what it means to live as the people of God in the
    world.
